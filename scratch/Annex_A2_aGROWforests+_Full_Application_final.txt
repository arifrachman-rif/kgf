2.1 Description of the Action
2.1.1 Description
In 2025, deforestation in Aceh surged by 274% in a single year. Flooding and landslides followed. The Global Centre on Adaptation (2023) estimates annual climate-related economic losses of USD 2–3 billion for smallholders in Sumatra. In Bangka Belitung, mining expansion is encroaching on pepper farming communities that have been cultivating the world's most prized white pepper for generations. In Lampung, coffee and cocoa farmers earn incomes persistently below the living income threshold, while their ageing tree stock continues to decline in productivity. These are not isolated crises, they are the predictable consequence of commodity landscapes where farmers have been trained but not governed, supported but not financed, and measured but not verified. The action addresses four interrelated systemic barriers simultaneously. 

At the same time, exporters and EU buyers face increasing obligations under the EU Deforestation Regulation (EUDR) and the Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive (CSDDD), requiring verifiable deforestation-free and climate-responsible sourcing. Smallholders without geolocation data, land documentation, traceability and agroforestry training risk exclusion from EU-oriented value chains. Indonesia's policy direction reinforces the need for integrated responses: Indonesia’s FOLU Net Sink 2030 goal, updated NDC and RPJMN 2025–2029 prioritise agroforestry-based rehabilitation, verified monitoring and strengthened supply chain governance,  yet practical delivery models, finance pathways and low-connectivity monitoring tools remain underdeveloped.

This action consolidates and scales up learnings from GIZ-funded aGROWforests project (agroforestry adoption at pepper farms) and EU-ECS (civil society capacity and participation in sustainable land-use governance). Agroforestry can reduce flood and landslide risks, but when surrounding land remains dominated by monocultures, communities are still highly exposed to climate shocks, so resilience requires a landscape approach. At the same time, landscape governance alone does not create a strong business case for farmers. By integrating these programmes under a Sustainable Consumption and Production (SCP) approach, the action combines landscape stewardship, greener value chains (resource efficiency, traceability, market access), and practical finance mechanisms to support climate resilience, nature-positive production, and climate-smart agriculture at scale.

2.1.1.2  Relevance to the SWITCH-Asia Call Objectives and Priorities
aGROWforests+ directly addresses all three specific objectives of the SWITCH-Asia call and the Global Gateway principles:
 
Specific Objective 1 — Green transformation of key value chains
Pepper, coffee and cocoa are strategic Indonesian export commodities to the EU facing heightened scrutiny related to deforestation, biodiversity loss, soil degradation and climate impact. The action supports the transition from monoculture to agroforestry and regenerative practices across 5,000 farms, combined with farm-to-fork traceability that generates verifiable deforestation-free supply chain evidence. This directly contributes to Verstegen's publicly committed 100% deforestation-free supply chain target (December 2025) and its goal of 50% sustainably grown agricultural raw materials by 2030, as well as to Sucafina's ongoing EUDR compliance work in Central Aceh.
 
Specific Objective 2 — Business environment and innovative financing models
The action co-develops local land-use rules (participatory ILM plans and community by-laws), practical green sourcing models and a Landscape Finance Approach (LFA) mechanism aligned with TNFD and SBTN frameworks. Together these reduce investment uncertainty and create improved conditions for private and blended finance to support nature-positive production. The action explicitly targets Development Finance Institutions (DFIs), voluntary carbon markets and biodiversity credit mechanisms as additional and complementary finance channels, directly responding to the call's emphasis on leveraging EU grant funding through innovative financing.
 
Specific Objective 3 — Empowering consumers to act more responsibly
Credible farm-to-fork traceability and MRV data, including Verstegen's blockchain transparency approach and Sucafina's geolocation traceability system, support consumer-facing impact claims and responsible purchasing decisions for European spice and coffee consumers. The verified performance data generated enables market actors to substantiate deforestation-free and climate-smart sourcing claims, directly linking informed consumer demand with on-the-ground production change in Sumatra.
 
Global Gateway Alignment
The action operationalises Global Gateway principles by: (i) fostering EU–private sector collaboration through an EU-Indonesian business consortium connecting Dutch buyers with Indonesian farmer organisations, MSMEs and exporters; (ii) developing an innovative Landscape Finance Approach mechanism that leverages the EU grant to mobilise private and blended capital; and (iii) applying a policy-based approach by embedding multi-actor governance, EUDR-aligned traceability and MRV frameworks within national planning systems (FOLU Net Sink 2030, RPJMN 2025–2029).
 
2.1.1.3  Alignment with Indonesian National Plans and Policy Priorities

The action operationalises several Indonesian policy priorities:
 
FOLU Net Sink 2030 and updated NDC: agroforestry-based rehabilitation and verified MRV data contribute to national GHG reporting requirements.
Social forestry and agroforestry regulations: the action strengthens land-use planning, tenure mapping, safeguards and productive agroforestry with social forestry groups.
RPJMN 2025–2029 (Perpres No. 12/2025): pepper (including Bangka-Belitung and Lampung Geographical Indications), coffee and cocoa are listed as strategic plantation commodities for strengthening downstreaming and competitiveness — directly aligned with the action's target value chains.
Indonesia's National Spice Downstreaming Roadmap 2025-2045: Farmer training on the pepper post harvest processing is a key to the quality improvement and enhanced Indonesia’s pepper product competitiveness in the global market. 
EUDR readiness: smallholder geolocation, traceability and legality documentation developed by the action are aligned with EU deforestation-free requirements, supporting Indonesia's national EUDR readiness agenda.
BAPPENAS through its Directorate of Forestry and Water Resource Conservation has formally endorsed aGROWforests+ as stated in the event of Pepper Agroforestry Workshop  which was hosted by Bappenas in February 2026. BAPPENAS recognises the action's integrated approach, combining agroforestry, biodiversity conservation, and economic profitability for smallholder farmers, as directly aligned with Indonesia's national development priorities, and specifically highlights the engagement of the private sector as a model for leveraging commercial incentives alongside public governance in commodity landscape management. Beyond formal endorsement, active collaboration between BAPPENAS and the existing aGROWforests programme is already underway. Ongoing discussions focus on extending the programme's agroforestry demonstration plots, strengthening their monitoring frameworks, and using the field evidence they generate as an input into Indonesian government policy design for climate-smart spice landscape restoration. 
The project will coordinate with relevant ministries (Environment and Forestry, Agriculture, National Development Planning/BAPPENAS), provincial agencies and district governments to ensure that landscape plans and MRV frameworks are compatible with existing planning tools and monitoring systems.
2.1.1.4  Synergies and Avoidance of Duplication
aGROWforests+ builds explicitly on two ongoing programmes, filling identified gaps rather than duplicating them:
 
aGROWforests for sustainable spices in Indonesia (GIZ-funded/I4C, Dec 2022 - May 2027)
Proven agroforestry models at pilot scale in Lampung and Bangka/Belitung — including the Change Maker community agronomist approach, Spice Hubs, the Kelekak agroforestry demo farm, structured nurseries, women's empowerment programmes and farm-to-fork traceability (agrowforests.id). The programme identified four structural gaps that aGROWforests+ directly addresses: landscape governance, green sourcing models, access to finance, and verified performance systems. While agroforestry reduces flood and landslide risk at farm level, communities remain vulnerable as long as surrounding landscapes are dominated by monocultures — requiring the landscape approach this action adopts.
 
EU-ECS (Enabling Civil Society, Feb 2024- Jan 2027)
Built CSO capacity for analysis, reporting and advocacy on sustainable land use across Sumatra. aGROWforests+ builds on this by embedding these CSOs in governance forums, accountability mechanisms and policy dialogue — rather than duplicating their capacity-building work.
 
Next to that aGROWforests+ is complementarity to other active programmes in the same landscapes:
 
FOLUR (Food Systems, Land Use & Restoration): aGROWforests+ adds the pepper pathway alongside coffee/cocoa landscapes and pilots a practical results-based payment mechanism with low-connectivity MRV methods suited to smallholders — addressing gaps not covered by FOLUR.
Social forestry support initiatives: The action strengthens tenure mapping, community governance and safeguards in target landscapes, reinforcing rather than duplicating social forestry programme activities.Conservation and livelihood programmes in Aceh and Lampung (including WWF-supported work): Landscape plans and agroforestry models reinforce conservation and tenure outcomes in overlapping districts, with WWF Indonesia's existing relationships ensuring alignment.
EU and broader deforestation-free value chain initiatives: The action provides inclusive field models for geolocation, traceability and legality evidence, and credible performance reporting for smallholders — the implementation layer that broader policy and finance initiatives depend on.
 
During the inception phase, the action will: (i) map active programmes per district and agree a clear division of roles in each landscape; (ii) reuse existing baselines, training materials and platforms where appropriate; (iii) align MRV and geolocation protocols with recognised standards and relevant national systems; and (iv) share non-sensitive indicators and lessons through existing multi-stakeholder and government coordination channels.
 
2.1.1.5  Target Groups and Final Beneficiaries

The action targets four interconnected stakeholder groups whose simultaneous engagement is essential for systemic change:
 
Smallholder farmers (5,000): pepper, coffee and cocoa smallholders across six communities in Sumatra (two in Bangka/Belitung, two in Lampung, two in Aceh).
Selection criteria: producing target commodities in programme areas; willingness to adopt agroforestry and regenerative practices and participate in training and monitoring.
Prioritisation: women farmers and women-led groups (minimum 20%); youth under 35 (minimum 15%); farmers in higher climate-risk areas; farmers linked to MSMEs supplying EU-oriented chains.
Needs: knowledge about climate change effects, practical support to adopt agroforestry practices, practical support to adapt to climate change/, access to quality inputs and planting material, clearer incentives and predictable market access, financial literacy and low-threshold financial access. 

MSMEs and supply-chain actors: local processors, aggregators and exporters connected to EU markets, specifically PT CAN (Bangka/Belitung pepper), PT KGF (Lampung pepper and cocoa) and Sucafina Indonesia (Aceh Arabica coffee).
Selection criteria: commitment to traceability and EUDR compliance; willingness to invest in green value chains, capacity and willingness to engage in longer-term sourcing models.
Needs: compliance-ready traceability and performance evidence, viable sourcing models that maintain supply quality while reducing compliance risk, credible data for consumer and regulatory reporting, a viable business model for sustainability. 

Local authorities: village, district and provincial bodies responsible for land-use planning, by-laws, enforcement and extension.
Selection criteria: mandate in target landscapes; willingness to co-develop participatory ILM plans and practical enforcement pathways.
Needs: practical planning tools, clearer tenure information, workable by-laws and enforcement pathways aligned with national policy.
 
CSOs and research/technical partners: organisations engaged in governance, inclusion, accountability and monitoring,  including EU-ECS-linked CSOs.
Selection criteria: ability to support independent analysis, safeguards and evidence-based policy dialogue.
Needs: structured roles and access to credible evidence to support community voice, safeguards and accountability mechanisms.

Final Beneficiaries - Approximately 48,000 community members across the six target communities in Bangka/Belitung, Lampung and Aceh will benefit indirectly from the action. Participatory landscape plans and community by-laws, combined with practical measures such as erosion control, riparian buffers, water retention and infiltration works and green corridors, will reduce flood and landslide risk, improve local water conditions and lower land-use conflict. Shared assets and services remain available to the wider community beyond the project period. Local implementation also generates spillover benefits through jobs and service demand in seedlings, labour, aggregation, processing and data roles.
 
2.1.1.6  Key Stakeholder Groups: Attitudes and Consultations
The design of aGROWforests+ is grounded in extensive stakeholder engagement conducted through the two ongoing predecessor programmes (aGROWforests and EU-ECS), rather than in a standalone consultation process. This provides a well-tested evidence base for the action's assumptions.
 
Smallholder Farmers - Farmer focus groups conducted through the aGROWforests programme in Bangka/Belitung and Lampung confirm strong interest in agroforestry adoption when: (i) quality inputs and planting material are reliably available; (ii) the connection to better market prices and more stable buyers is visible; and (iii) the transition period, during which income from monoculture may temporarily decline, is supported through practical incentives. Farmers are cautious about practices that require significant upfront labour investment without a clear short-term return. This feedback directly shaped the action's integration of results-based finance mechanisms and the decision to provide inputs, nurseries and Change Maker agronomist support alongside training.
 
Local Authorities - Bilateral discussions with village, district and provincial government offices in the target landscapes confirmed that authorities value tools that strengthen planning, by-laws and enforcement while supporting local development priorities. Key concerns include the risk of tenure conflicts during land-use change processes and the need for approaches that align with,  rather than bypass,  national planning frameworks. These consultations shaped the action's commitment to embedding landscape plans within formal governance structures and aligning MRV frameworks with existing national monitoring systems.
 
Supply Chain Actors (MSMEs and EU Buyers) - Targeted conversations with PT CAN, PT KGF, Sucafina Indonesia, Verstegen and other supply chain actors validated both the urgency of EUDR compliance needs and the willingness to engage in longer-term sourcing models when credible data and governance structures are in place. Exporters and EU buyers recognise rising compliance and disclosure expectations and expressed clear demand for supply chains with verifiable deforestation-free evidence, traceable origin data and credible performance claims on climate and biodiversity. This feedback confirmed the commercial viability of the action's green value chain model.
 
NGOs and Technical Partners - Engagement through the EU-ECS programme established a network of CSOs with demonstrated capacity for landscape analysis, reporting and advocacy. These organisations support inclusive governance and safeguards and expressed strong interest in structured roles within a programme that provides credible evidence for policy dialogue. WWF Indonesia's participation was shaped by their ongoing work in the Peusangan corridor (Aceh) and Lampung landscapes, where the alignment between conservation priorities and the action's ILM approach was explicitly confirmed through programme coordination meetings.

2.1.1.9 aGROWforests+ intervention logic
The action adopts an integrated landscape and value-chain approach. It is a field-tested, integrated system that simultaneously addresses the four root causes of failed commodity sustainability initiatives: fragmented land governance, missing market incentives, absent finance mechanisms, and unverifiable performance claims. By combining a Landscape Governance (Pillar A), a Nature-Positive Value Chain (pillar B), Results-Based Finance (pillar C), and a Digital MRV system (Pillar D) into one coherent programme, the action demonstrates that ecology and economy are not in conflict, they are mutually reinforcing when the right institutions, incentives, and evidence systems are in place. This section sets out the intervention logic that ties these four pillars together, the results chain from inputs to impact, and the case for why this integrated framework is both credible, built on existing proven experience, and scalable to other landscapes and commodities across Southeast Asia.  

From activities to outputs
The following describes how the activities, if implemented and if stated assumptions hold, lead to the production of each output. Activities are clustered by output in line with the Activities Matrix (Annex C).

Output 1.1 – Three landscape analyses and six multi-functional landscape plans 
WWF Indonesia leads landscape governance activities in all three regions, coordinating field analysis and multi-stakeholder engagement. Fairfood International coordinates cross-consortium data integration and overall project management. PT CAN and PT KGF support data collection and stakeholder engagement in Bangka/Belitung and Lampung respectively; Sucafina Indonesia supports data collection in Aceh.
Activities and justification
Act. 1.1.1 – Baseline and Climate Risk Assessment: Review historical climate and land-use trends to understand emissions, land-use change, and climate risks in each landscape. Identify climate adaptation needs for pepper, coffee, and cocoa systems and suitable diversification options. This activity is essential because landscape plans that do not account for compound climate risks, floods, droughts, peat fire risk, will be overtaken by events before the project ends. The CRA provides the analytical foundation for all subsequent landscape planning decisions and directly informs which agroforestry species and practices are recommended to farmers in each region.
Act. 1.1.2 – Participatory land-use mapping: Map farming areas, conservation zones, water resources, and ecological corridors together with communities and local authorities. Participatory mapping is chosen over remote sensing-only approaches because it integrates local knowledge of customary boundaries, informal land use, and contested areas that satellite data cannot capture, and because the act of joint mapping builds community ownership of the resulting landscape plans. Results directly inform the six landscape plans.
Act. 1.1.3 – Social analysis: Identify tenure boundaries, customary rights, and social forestry areas, reducing exclusion risk. Integrate tenure safeguards into landscape plans. This activity is included because the predecessor EU-ECS programme identified unresolved tenure as the primary driver of land-use conflict in target landscapes. Without explicit tenure mapping, landscape plans risk formalising existing inequities or triggering disputes that halt implementation. 
Act. 1.1.4 – Establish multi-stakeholder forums: Set up governance forums in six target communities with local government, farmers, CSOs, and private sector actors. These forums provide the institutional home for planning, coordination, and adaptive management throughout the project and beyond. They will be established in the inception phase because the landscape plans themselves need to be co-developed with the forums to have legitimacy.
Act. 1.1.5 – Develop landscape plans: Develop six participatory landscape plans using climate, mapping, and tenure data. Define cultivation zones, conservation areas, restoration priorities, and water management measures. These plans are the primary governance output of Outcome 1 and the reference document against which community by-laws, land-use measures, and ILM forum decisions are assessed throughout the project. 

Output 1.2 – Community by-laws and land-use measures agreed through multi-actor forums
WWF Indonesia leads community by-law development and ILM forum facilitation in all three regions. PT KGF supports by-law development in Lampung; PT CAN supports by-law development in Bangka/Belitung. WWF Indonesia and Fairfood International co-author the policy alignment report.
Activities and justification
Act. 1.2.1 – Develop community by-laws: Support communities and local authorities to formalise land-use agreements and by-laws based on the landscape plans. By-laws are chosen as the formalisation instrument because they are legally recognised under Indonesia's Village Law (UU No. 6/2014), can be adopted within the project timeline through existing local government processes, and provide an enforcement mechanism for land-use restrictions that informal agreements cannot. This directly addresses the gap identified in aGROWforests, where individual farmer adoption of agroforestry was undermined by unchecked conversion on neighbouring land.
Act. 1.2.2 – Design land-use measures: Establish plans for green corridors, riparian buffers, and water retention infrastructure agreed through the forums. These physical measures translate the landscape plans into on-the-ground action and reduce flood, landslide, and water stress risk for farming communities. They are co-designed through ILM forums to ensure community co-ownership and shared maintenance responsibility beyond the project period.
Act. 1.2.3 – Strengthen ILM forums: Hold regular forum meetings to review progress, discuss monitoring data, and update plans where needed. Support forums to continue beyond the project period. Ongoing forum strengthening is essential because a forum established during inception but not sustained through implementation will not produce lasting governance change. Regular meetings ensure that MRV data flows back into adaptive management decisions, creating a feedback loop between evidence and action.
Act. 1.2.4 – Publish policy alignment report: Document how community landscape plans align with Indonesian national policies and frameworks (FOLU Net Sink 2030, NDC, RPJMN 2025–2029). Share findings with government stakeholders. This report serves two purposes: it demonstrates to national and provincial authorities that the project's governance outputs are compatible with and support national commitments, increasing the likelihood of institutional adoption; and it creates a replicable model document for other landscapes.

Output 2.1 – Farmer support package delivered 
PT KGF leads farmer training for 1,500 farmers in Lampung, including development and alignment of training materials, demo plot establishment, and nursery management. PT CAN conducts training for 1,500 farmers in Bangka/Belitung under the same structure. Sucafina Indonesia leads training for 2,000 farmers in Aceh, incorporating EUDR-compliant practices and regenerative agriculture. WWF Indonesia supports training material design to integrate the landscape approach. Fairfood coordinates cross-consortium learning and baseline/endline income analysis for Lampung and Bangka/Belitung.
Activities and justification
Act. 2.1.1 – Develop training programme: Develop climate-smart agriculture training materials for pepper, coffee, and cocoa farmers covering agroforestry, soil health, climate adaptation, and sustainable production standards. Target: Recruit and train 18-24 community-based Change Makers and field facilitators to support farmer learning, peer-to-peer extension, and digital data collection linked to the MRV system. This activity addresses limited technical knowledge and weak extension systems that constrain adoption of climate-smart practices. Local facilitators improve farmer trust, strengthen long-term adoption, and support credible field-level monitoring while linking training directly to market access and results-based finance opportunities.
Act. 2.1.2 – Establish demo plots and nurseries: Set up agroforestry demonstration plots and structured nurseries producing certified seedlings using biological agents. Target: at least six demo plots and nine nurseries across three regions. Demo plots are the most effective mechanism for farmer behaviour change in contexts where farmers are risk-averse about adopting new practices on their main productive land. Nurseries provide the physical supply of improved planting material and generate income that can sustain hub operations beyond the project period.
Act. 2.1.3 – Deliver farmer training and CSA interventions: Train 5,000 farmers in climate-smart agroforestry, soil health, climate adaptation, and pest management. Conduct farm and soil assessments to tailor training support. Training is the primary mechanism for farmer behaviour change and is directly linked to market access (Outcome 2) and results-based finance eligibility (Outcome 3), ensuring that the investment in training generates sustained commercial incentives rather than one-off knowledge transfer.
Act. 2.1.4 – Implement landscape restoration activities: Restore degraded land, establish agroforestry systems, and support riparian revegetation across project landscapes (Kelekak model in Bangka/Belitung; LKC Demo Farm ex-mine land rehabilitation in Lampung; Peusangan corridor in Aceh). Restoration activities are included because degraded land in the target landscapes represents both a climate and biodiversity risk and an economic opportunity, rehabilitated land can be brought into productive agroforestry systems that generate farmer income while sequestering carbon.
Act. 2.1.5 – Support post-harvest and micro-enterprises: Support farmer groups with composting, seedling production, and other income-generating activities linked to agroforestry systems. Post-harvest and enterprise support diversifies farmer income and reduces dependence on a single commodity price, directly addressing the income volatility risk identified in the context analysis. Composting and seedling production also create local supply of inputs that reduces the cost of agroforestry adoption for all farmers in the landscape.

Output 2.2 – Traceability applied to targeted value chains.
Fairfood International leads traceability platform development, deployment, and alignment across all regions. PT CAN, PT KGF, and Sucafina Indonesia each deploy the traceability system within their farmer networks and provide farm data. Verstegen and Sucafina provide traceability and certification data for product volumes sourced from and sold to EU buyers.
Activities and justification
Act. 2.2.1 – Traceability system deployment: Deploy digital tools for farm registration, geolocation, and product tracking. EU buyers of selected commodities, including coffee and cocoa, require EUDR-ready documentation for market access. Low-tech tools (paper forms, offline mobile apps, SMS-based reporting, periodic synchronisation) are deployed for low-connectivity areas to ensure that remote farmers are not excluded from the deforestation-free evidence system. The system integrates with Sucafina's, Verstegen’s, PT CAN’s and PT KGF’s existing data collection infrastructure to avoid duplication. .
Act. 2.2.2 – Scale traceability system: Roll out the traceability system across all regions and value chains. Register at least 3,000 farm plots with geolocation data. MSMEs, processors, and exporters trained and onboarded to the traceability platform. Scaling to 3,000 plots is necessary to cover a commercially significant volume of production across the three regions and to provide the statistical basis for deforestation-free claims at the supply chain level. MSME and exporter onboarding ensures that the chain of custody is maintained from farm to export, which is the evidentiary standard required under EUDR.

Output 2.3 – Documented sourcing arrangements and business models for greener value chains
Verstegen leads the development of one green sourcing agreement for pepper. Sucafina leads the development of two green sourcing agreements for coffee. Fairfood coordinates buyer engagement across all commodities and supports the development of consumer-facing impact communication for all commodity chains.
Activities and justification
Act. 2.3.1 – Engage buyers and develop sourcing MoUs: Facilitate discussions between buyers, processors, and farmer groups on traceability requirements, living income pricing, and green sourcing expectations. Develop sourcing MoUs that formalise buyer intent at inception. MoUs are used at this stage because the traceability and certification infrastructure required for binding green sourcing claims is still being built during the first 12–18 months of the project. Formalising intent early ensures buyer commitment is in place before the investment in traceability is completed.
Act. 2.3.2 – Formalise sourcing agreements: Finalise binding green sourcing agreements for deforestation-free and agroforestry-produced commodities. Binding agreements are the instrument chosen because they create enforceable obligations that survive leadership changes and market fluctuations, providing the durability of market incentive that single-project training cycles cannot.

Output 2.4 – Documented evidence of income change for project-supported farmers
Fairfood coordinates income baseline and endline analysis for Lampung and Bangka/Belitung, including living income pricing analysis. Sucafina coordinates baseline and endline analysis for Aceh. PT CAN and PT KGF contribute to income monitoring and income-generating activities in their respective regions.
Activities and justification
Act. 2.4.1 – Conduct income baseline and monitoring: Conduct income baseline and analyse results by gender, age, and commodity. A rigorous baseline is essential for credibly measuring income change at the endline. Living Income Commodity Strategy methodology will be used to assess living income of the farmers and develop contact relevant interventions. 
Act. 2.4.2 – Track income improvements: Monitor changes in farmer income linked to agroforestry, productivity, market access, and finance mechanisms. Tracking income enables adaptive management: if income improvements are not materialising on track, the project can adjust interventions before it is too late to correct course.

Output 3.1 – Results-based finance mechanism (indicators, eligibility, payment rules, disbursement approach) linked to value chain demand and community landscape implementation needs.
Fairfood International leads the design and development of the results-based finance mechanism and stakeholder engagement process. WWF Indonesia supports the design, specifically on the landscape component and MRV alignment. Verstegen and Sucafina provide buyer-side input to ensure the mechanism is anchored to commercial demand.
Activities and justification
Act. 3.1.1 – Design ecosystem services scoring framework: Develop a scoring framework linking farm and landscape performance to payment eligibility. A scoring framework is chosen because it allows partial achievement to be rewarded, reducing the risk that farmers who make genuine progress but do not meet a single threshold receive nothing. The scoring framework directly uses MRV system outputs (Outcome 4), creating the evidence-to-incentive link that is the mechanism's core design principle.
Act. 3.1.2 – Conduct financial feasibility analysis: Assess financial models, premium pricing, and blended finance opportunities. Identify potential investors and finance partners. A rigorous feasibility analysis is essential before the mechanism is piloted to avoid designing a payment structure that is commercially unsustainable or that fails to meet investor due diligence requirements. The feasibility analysis is the basis for approaching DFIs and impact investors with a credible co-financing proposition.
Act. 3.1.3 – Pilot finance mechanism: Pilot the one finance mechanism and refine the mechanism based on lessons learned. A pilot payment cycle is included because a mechanism that is designed but never tested carries no evidence of operational feasibility, the endorsement and piloting sequence ensures that the design document produced as Output 3.1 reflects a mechanism that has been validated in practice, not just on paper.


Output 4.1 – Operational MRV system with protocols, data quality checks, dashboards, verification steps, and data responsibility procedures
Fairfood International leads the design, development, and operation of the MRV system, including all protocols, dashboards, and verification procedures. WWF Indonesia supports MRV design for the landscape component. PT CAN, PT KGF, and Sucafina Indonesia provide farm data across their respective regions.
Activities and justification
Act. 4.1.1 – Develop MRV framework and protocols: Develop indicators and measurement protocols for climate, biodiversity, land use, and socio-economic performance. Align with EUDR and other relevant frameworks. The multi-dimensional scope of the MRV framework reflects the programme's holistic ambition: lasting impact requires verified evidence across climate, biodiversity, land-use, and socio-economic dimensions simultaneously. A single-metric system would capture only part of the transformation and fail to support the results-based finance mechanism, whose payment triggers depend on cross-dimensional performance data to credibly reward farmers and attract blended finance
Act. 4.1.2 – Implement data collection tools: Implement offline and low-connectivity data collection tools. Requiring smartphone connectivity or reliable internet access for data collection would exclude the majority of target farmers and compromise data coverage. The tools are designed to be operated by trained community data agents rather than technical specialists, ensuring operational sustainability.
Act. 4.1.3 – Register farmers and conduct baseline surveys: Register farm plots and collect baseline data across all target communities. Establish data governance procedures. Baseline registration in the inception phase is essential because the results-based finance mechanism (Outcome 3) requires a verified baseline against which performance improvements are measured, a mechanism designed without a baseline cannot credibly pay for verified improvement.
Act. 4.1.4 – Operate MRV system and dashboards: Deploy dashboards and conduct annual verification and data quality checks. Dashboards designed for different user groups, and quality data through checks ensure that MRV data is actually used in decision-making rather than sitting in a database.
Act. 4.1.5 – Reporting: Prepare interim and final technical and financial reports using MRV and monitoring data. The MRV system is the primary source for all indicator tracking, ensuring that project reports to the contracting authority are based on verified field data rather than estimates.

2.1.1.10 Context and location 
aGROWforests+ operates across three distinct landscapes in Sumatra, Indonesia: Bangka Belitung, Lampung, and Aceh. Each area has its own agricultural context, commodity focus, and stage of development, and the project's approach is tailored accordingly. The sections below describe the current situation, existing foundations, and planned interventions for each location.

Bangka Belitung - Target group: 1.500 farmers, Key commodities: White pepper, patchouli, coffee.
The project builds on PT CAN’s existing work with over 1,300 pepper farmers across ten Spice Hubs established under the original aGROWforests initiative. These community-based hubs, led by trained Change Makers, connect farmers to markets while strengthening climate-smart agricultural skills. Activities include farmer training, nursery management using Trichoderma and PGPR, biological agent production through soil labs, organic fertilizer and compost production, post-harvest value addition, and market coordination. Farmers mainly cultivate Muntok White Pepper, alongside coffee, patchouli, aromatic ginger, and organic fertilizer production for additional income. 

The landscape already has a strong Pillar B foundation (nature-positive value chains) but lacks landscape governance (Pillar A), results-based finance (Pillar C), and digital MRV systems (Pillar D). Without community land-use agreements, mining expansion and forest conversion continue to threaten agroforestry investments. The project will therefore establish ILM governance forums, participatory land-use planning, community by-laws, results-based finance linked to ecosystem performance, and low-connectivity digital MRV tools, while further strengthening the Spice Hub model through expanded climate-smart agriculture training. 

Sustainability is supported through income-generating Spice Hubs, farmer-to-farmer learning networks, established market linkages, and blended finance partnerships with the SOLUSI (IKI) and Blue Economy (YKAN) projects. Key partners are PT CAN (farmer enterprise development and community engagement), Fairfood International (coordination, traceability, and Landscape Finance Approach design), WWF Indonesia (landscape governance and ILM forums), and Verstegen Spices & Sauces (EU market anchor for deforestation-free pepper sourcing). Of the 1,500 target farmers, around 1,100 come from the existing programme and 400 are newly enrolled from adjacent communities.

Lampung - Target group: 1.500 farmers, Key commodities: Black pepper, cacao, coffee.
In Lampung, the project builds on PT KGF’s engagement with more than 1,250 smallholder farmers in Tanggamus and Pesawaran. Three Village Hubs already provide training, input distribution, coordination, and market access. Existing infrastructure includes a certified pepper mother plant farm, a composting facility producing 20 tonnes per six-month cycle, nursery networks across eight villages with capacities of 3,000–5,000 seedlings each, and the LKC Demo Farm for ex-mine land rehabilitation and agroforestry education. 

Black pepper is the main commodity, combined with coffee and cocoa to diversify incomes. The landscape already has a strong Pillar B base through training systems, demo farms, nurseries, composting facilities, and field monitoring, but still lacks Pillars A, C, and D. Farmers face climate variability, unstable prices, limited access to quality inputs and finance, and weak bargaining power. The project will therefore establish participatory ILM forums and community land-use planning, pilot results-based finance linked to environmental and social performance, and deploy low-tech digital MRV tools, while expanding climate-smart agriculture training, pest and disease management, and post-harvest improvements. 

A key new intervention is a cocoa fermentation hub in Pesawaran linked to a district-owned mini chocolate factory capable of producing chocolate bars, cocoa powder, and cocoa butter, enabling greater local value capture. Sustainability is anchored in locally owned Village Hubs, nurseries, compost units, trained facilitators, the fermentation hub, and the chocolate factory partnership, supported by revenues from seedlings, compost, pepper, coffee, fermented cocoa, and chocolate products. Main partners are PT KGF (training, demo farms, and compost enterprise development), Fairfood International (traceability and Landscape Finance Approach coordination), and WWF Indonesia (landscape governance and ILM forums). Of the 1,500 target farmers, around 1,000 are from the existing programme and 500 are newly enrolled.

Aceh - Target group: 2.000 farmers, Key commodities: Coffee
In Aceh, the project supports 2,000 smallholder Arabica coffee farmers in Central Aceh and Bener Meriah, organised through cooperatives within Sucafina’s direct supply chain. Farmers typically manage 1–2 hectare plots at elevations of 1,200–1,500 metres, producing 800–1,500 kg of green coffee annually. Arabica coffee is the main household income source. 

The region faces major challenges: deforestation reached an estimated 39,687 hectares in 2025, over 274% higher than in 2024, largely driven by coffee expansion and illegal logging; coffee trees aged 15–25 years are producing lower yields; incomes remain below Fairtrade living income benchmarks; and erratic La Niña rainfall has reduced productivity by 20%. Unlike Bangka Belitung and Lampung, Aceh requires simultaneous development across all four pillars. 

Sucafina’s regional agronomy team leads Pillar B through regenerative agriculture, agroforestry design, climate-smart farm management, and supply chain decarbonisation, supported by geolocation traceability and partially operational EUDR-compliant deforestation analysis. WWF Indonesia leads Pillar A through the Peusangan Elephant Conservation Initiative (PECI), integrating elephant corridor protection, biodiversity conservation, and community livelihoods into ILM forum development. Fairfood International leads Pillar D (digital MRV) and Pillar C (Landscape Finance Approach), translating verified environmental performance into buyer premiums and blended finance opportunities. 

Sustainability is reinforced through Sucafina’s long-term sourcing commitments, cooperative-based market access and financial services, farm management plans, and a deforestation-free, EUDR-compliant supply chain that creates lasting incentives for sustainable production. Active partners are Sucafina Indonesia (training, traceability, and EUDR compliance), WWF Indonesia (landscape governance and ILM forums), and Fairfood International (MRV, Landscape Finance Approach, and project coordination).




2.1.1.11 Mainstreaming Cross-Cutting Issues

Gender equality and social inclusion: Gender equality is embedded in the programme's design, not treated as an add-on. The minimum targets, 25% women among trained farmers and 15% youth under 35, are supported by specific structural measures. PT KGF's women's farmer empowerment programme, covering seedling production, compost enterprise management, and post-harvest processing, is formally integrated into the consortium's training curriculum across all three regions. In Aceh, Sucafina's farmer group structure includes women's cooperative sub-groups through which training, income monitoring, and input distribution are channelled. In Bangka Belitung, PT CAN's Spice Hub model explicitly recruits women as Change Makers and nursery managers.
Practical inclusion measures include: scheduling training sessions at times compatible with domestic responsibilities; providing childcare support at training venues where feasible; ensuring women are represented in ILM forum governance structures with a minimum of 30% of forum seats; and designing income monitoring tools that capture women's independent income separately from household income to make women's economic contributions visible.
All indicators are disaggregated by gender and age in the MRV system. An annual gender-sensitive income analysis will track whether women and youth are proportionally benefiting from income improvements and results-based payments. Where gaps are identified, corrective measures are triggered within one adaptive management cycle.
Indigenous Peoples and customary rights: Tenure mapping explicitly identifies customary rights where Indigenous groups are present. The Peusangan corridor case study in Aceh — where ILM resolved Human-Elephant Conflict by integrating elephant habitat conservation with coffee agroforestry — demonstrates the approach's capacity to reconcile conservation, customary rights, and livelihoods. Participatory planning processes include safeguards and accessible grievance mechanisms.
Environmental and climate change integration: Landscape plans integrate biodiversity, soil health, water management, and GHG mitigation. CRA analyses compound climate risks (floods, droughts, peat fires where applicable) and recommends adaptive commodities and interventions based on 20-year climate trajectories. The action avoids significant adverse effects on the environment and does not involve financial instruments ineligible under the call.
Carbon sequestration and land restoration as a co-benefit of agroforestry: Transitioning degraded smallholder farms from monoculture systems to productive agroforestry is a central delivery mechanism of aGROWforests+. This directly supports the SWITCH-Asia call's objective of reducing the carbon and environmental footprint of key value chains, and operationalises Indonesia's FOLU Net Sink 2030 commitment and updated NDC, which prioritise agroforestry-based land rehabilitation and verified monitoring at landscape scale.
The agroforestry models applied across the three target regions are designed around three mutually reinforcing goals: improving soil health and structure, strengthening farm-level climate resilience, and demonstrating the economic viability of nature-positive production. This last point is deliberate. Field experience from the predecessor aGROWforests project demonstrates that long-term farmer adoption depends not on training alone, but on farmers seeing a credible and sustained business case. Agroforestry that pays is agroforestry that endures.
A significant co-benefit of this approach is carbon sequestration. As part of the original aGROWforests project, a calculation model was developed to estimate the carbon removal potential of tree species distributed to farmers, projected across both the project period and a ten-year horizon beyond planting. 
It is important to note that the model intentionally excludes species with negligible sequestration capacity. The figures presented therefore represent a conservative estimate of the programme's climate contribution, limited to species with meaningful CO₂e removal trajectories.

2.1.2 Methodology 

2.1.2.1  Integrated Methodology
aGROWforests+ operates through a single integrated methodology that weaves together four approaches: (i) Integrated Landscape Management (ILM), (ii) climate-smart agroforestry, (iii) nature-positive value chain development, and the (iv) Landscape Finance Approach, into one coherent system. What makes this programme distinct is not any one of these approaches in isolation, but the way they are designed to reinforce each other, and the active, contractual roles assigned to private sector actors throughout. The sections below describe each approach, how they integrate, and how the programme is governed, monitored, and communicated.

The core argument: Why single-pillar approaches fail, and how integration succeeds
The global track record of commodity sustainability programmes reveals a persistent pattern of failure: interventions that address only one dimension of the problem achieve temporary results that unravel once the project ends. Farmer training without market access leaves knowledge without incentive. Market access without landscape governance exposes supply chains to continuing environmental risk (like floods), land-use conflict, and deforestation encroachment that undermines their investment. Finance without verified evidence funds greenwashing rather than impact. Evidence without connected finance systems generates reports that sit unused. aGROWforests+ is designed to break this pattern. Its central proposition is that durable, nature-positive transformation of commodity landscapes requires all four pillars to be operational simultaneously, and that each pillar actively strengthens the others. This integrated logic is not theoretical, it is grounded in direct lessons from two existing programmes.

Building on Existing Experience: From Gap Analysis to Integrated Design
The design of aGROWforests+ is directly derived from a systematic analysis of gaps identified in the two predecessor programmes. The table below shows how each gap maps to a specific pillar of the integrated framework, and why the integration is essential rather than additive.


This gap analysis produces a critical insight: the four pillars are not additional components added to make the project more comprehensive, they are each necessary conditions for the others to deliver lasting impact. Remove any one pillar and the system breaks down. Add all four and they create a self-reinforcing system that can survive beyond the grant period, attract private investment, and generate the verified evidence needed for replication. 
Based on this analysis, aGROWforests+ combines four mutually reinforcing pillars implemented simultaneously at landscape level.

Integrated Landscape Management (ILM)
A sustainable landscape approach is a conceptual framework designed to reconcile competing social, economic, and environmental objectives by moving away from unsustainable sectoral land management. It balances local needs such as water security, biodiversity, and food income with global goals like GHG emission reductions and conservation targets. When stakeholders collaborate through this framework, it is termed Integrated Landscape Management (ILM). To achieve a sustainable landscape the program needs to implement the following five elements of ILM systematically:
Multi-stakeholder platform: A structured governance forum bringing together government, private sector, and farmer  groups to synchronise land-use policies and  decisions across the whole landscape, not just individual farms.
Shared understanding: A common evidence base built from Business-as-Usual (BaU) baselining, Climate Risk Assessment (CRA), and spatial mapping, to align on landscape conditions, challenges, and opportunities using data-driven evidence.
Collaborative planning: Participatory development of multi-functional landscape plans that define cultivation zones, conservation areas, water management corridors, and restoration priorities — co-owned by communities and authorities.
Effective implementation: Execution of the plan through community by-laws, green corridors, riparian buffers, water retention infrastructure, and the Change Maker and Spice Hub delivery models, sustained through strong governance and robust communication.
Monitoring for Adaptive Management: A transparent MRV system that assesses progress, feeds verified data back into ILM forum decision-making, and adjusts interventions based on dynamic field conditions.
Climate-Smart Agroforestry Implementation
Within the ILM governance framework, on-the-ground implementation is delivered through a climate-smart agroforestry (CSA) model that combines on-farm and off-farm interventions. The agroforestry approach targets three outputs simultaneously aligned with the Climate Smart Agriculture framework: productivity (increasing yields to support income and food security), climate adaptation (building resilient systems against floods and droughts), and climate mitigation (reducing GHG emissions through soil management, shade canopy, and restoration).
Before any field activities begin, a participatory design process ensures long-term sustainability and social equity through three principles: (1) inclusive consultation engaging vulnerable groups, women, and youth; (2) integration of local wisdom — the Kelekak mixed-farming model in Bangka/Belitung, and the Peusangan Mutual Benefit System integrating elephant habitat conservation with coffee agroforestry in Aceh; and (3) explicit consideration of projected climate change in all activity and species selection decisions, avoiding maladaptation.
Five delivery models operationalise the agroforestry approach:
Change Makers & Field facilitators: Locally recruited community agronomist trainers, trained in CSA techniques, gender-sensitive facilitation, and MRV data collection. Proven in the predecessor aGROWforests project to be more effective than external extension because they are trusted by farmers, available year-round, and remain as community champions after the project ends. Approximately 18–24 Change Makers trained across the three regions.
Spice Hubs and Village Hubs: Community advisory centres providing technical expertise, certified inputs, and market information. PT CAN operates ten Spice Hubs in Bangka/Belitung; PT KGF operates Village Hubs in Lampung; new cooperative-linked hubs established in Aceh. Hub infrastructure includes soil testing kits, biological agent production units (Trichoderma, PGPR, Metarhizium), composting facilities, and certified nursery infrastructure.
Agroforestry demo plots and demo farms: At least six demonstration sites across three regions — Kelekak model farms (Bangka/Belitung), Ngarip Ulubelu and LKC Demo Farm for ex-mine rehabilitation (Lampung), new sites (Aceh). Demo plots drive farmer behaviour change in risk-averse smallholder contexts: farmers adopt new practices significantly faster when they can observe yields and returns directly.
Structured nurseries: Certified seedling production using biological agents. PT KGF's eight-village network (3,000–5,000 seedlings each), PT KGF's certified mother plant farm (Taman Sari), and new nurseries in Aceh and Bangka/Belitung. At least nine nurseries operational by 2029. Nurseries generate income from seedling sales, providing a self-financing platform that sustains hub operations after the project ends.
Landscape restoration: Riparian revegetation, ex-mine rehabilitation, and green corridor establishment in each landscape, integrating on-farm agroforestry into broader land-use change. 65,000–70,000 shade trees distributed annually by Sucafina in Aceh, contributing to the programme's projected 1,126 tCO₂e carbon sequestration by 2030.
 
Nature-Positive Value Chain Approach and Private Sector Integration
A defining and distinguishing feature of aGROWforests+ is that private sector actors are not peripheral supporters, they are contractual consortium partners with direct delivery responsibilities and commercial obligations that sustain the programme beyond the grant period. This model deliberately closes the gap identified in the predecessor project, where the absence of durable market incentives caused farmers to revert to conventional practices after project support ended.
The value chain approach operates through three interconnected elements:
Farm-to-fork traceability: Fairfood International's digital transparency platform registers farm plots with EUDR-compliant polygon mapping and tracks products from farm to EU buyer. Low-tech tools (offline apps, paper forms, SMS synchronisation) ensure smallholders in low-connectivity areas are fully included. Sucafina's existing Aceh infrastructure is integrated rather than duplicated. Target: 3,000 farm plots registered, covering commercially significant volumes in all three regions.
Binding green sourcing agreements: Verstegen Spices & Sauces, a B Corp, SBTi-validated Dutch buyer with a publicly committed 100% deforestation-free pepper sourcing goal, provides the commercial anchor for the pepper value chain. Sucafina, with a direct supply network of 1,000+ farmers in Aceh since 2021 and a regenerative agriculture framework aligned with SBTi and living income commitments, provides the same for coffee. These are not aspirational sourcing intentions: they are pre-existing commercial relationships that are formalised into binding green sourcing agreements during implementation. Target: three documented green sourcing agreements by 2030.
Living income pricing and income monitoring: A living income pricing analysis provides the evidence base for buyer-farmer price negotiations, ensuring agreements include pricing that supports the 20% income increase target. Annual household income surveys track progress against the 2027 baseline, disaggregated by gender, age, and commodity.
 
The private sector partners are assigned their roles precisely because their institutional commitments, Verstegen's B Corp certification and SBTi targets, Sucafina's regenerative agriculture framework and EUDR compliance work, create obligations that exist independently of the EU grant. This is the mechanism that makes the programme's commercial sustainability architecture durable: the market incentive for farmers does not expire when the project ends.
Landscape Finance Approach (LFA)
The LFA, developed by WWF (2025) and aligned with TNFD and SBTN frameworks, bridges the funding gap between the EU grant and the private investment needed to sustain landscape stewardship long-term. It links ecosystem resilience (verified through the MRV system) and supply chain performance (verified through traceability) to financial returns for investors and commercial returns for buyers.
The Climate Risk Assessment quantifies landscape-level climate risk, making the target landscapes more attractive to institutional investors seeking nature-positive, low-risk assets. The ecosystem services scoring framework developed under Output 3.1 links verified farm and landscape performance indicators (canopy cover, species diversity, soil organic matter, water management, deforestation-free status) directly to payment eligibility. Buyer premiums from Verstegen and Sucafina anchor the mechanism at pilot scale; development finance institutions and impact investors are targeted for blended finance beyond the grant period. Target: EUR 300,000 in blended finance leveraged by 2030, scalable to EUR 1,000,000 by 2032.
 
2.1.2.2.  Organisational Structure
Day-to-day implementation is coordinated through a Project Management Unit (PMU) hosted at Fairfood International, transitioning to Solidaridad from July 2026 as described in Section 2.1.1.8. The PMU comprises a Project Director (overall accountability and EC contractual compliance), Project Coordinator (day-to-day management and partner coordination), Financial Manager (EU financial compliance, procurement, co-financing), MRV and Traceability Lead (system development and operation), LFA and Finance Mechanism Lead (results-based finance design and investor outreach), and EU Communication and Visibility Officer.
A Consortium Steering Committee, comprising senior representatives of all six partners, meets quarterly to review Logframe progress and provide strategic oversight. Regional Coordinators at PT CAN (Bangka/Belitung), PT KGF (Lampung), and Sucafina Indonesia (Aceh) manage field implementation and report to the PMU. WWF Indonesia's Landscape Governance Coordinator oversees ILM forum facilitation, landscape planning, and biodiversity monitoring across all three regions. Verstegen's Sustainability and Sourcing Lead coordinates buyer-side activities and EU consumer communication.
Weekly meetings will be held between all project partners to discuss progress, share challenges and lessons learned. 

2.1.2.3  Monitoring and Follow-Up
Monitoring operates at four nested levels, with data flowing from individual farmer to programme level through the MRV system and feeding back into ILM forum adaptive management decisions:
Farmer level: Change Makers collect training attendance, practice adoption surveys, input distribution records, and annual income data using offline mobile apps and paper forms, synchronised to the traceability platform and MRV system. All data disaggregated by gender and age.
Community level: WWF Indonesia field officers record ILM forum participation, by-law adoption, landscape plan progress, and biodiversity and land-use monitoring data. Forum minutes and signed attendance records maintained as primary evidence.
Value chain level: Traceability platform tracks sourced volumes, EUDR polygon deforestation status, and certification progress per commodity and region. Quarterly exports reviewed by the PMU and cross-referenced against sourcing agreement milestones.
Programme level: MRV dashboards aggregate all indicators against Logframe targets. Reviewed at each quarterly Steering Committee. Deviations from trajectory trigger an adaptive management response within 30 days.
Annual reports are submitted to the European Commission against the full Logframe matrix. All indicators are aligned with SDG goals and targets, the Global Europe Results Framework, and DG INTPA's Green Economy indicators.

2.1.4 Sustainability of the Action 
2.1.4.1  Long-Lasting Benefits
aGROWforests+ is designed to generate durable, multi-dimensional benefits that continue and compound after the grant period ends. The sustainability of these benefits operates at four interconnected levels.
Technical - Agroforestry systems, once established, deliver benefits over decades. The Kelekak model (Bangka/Belitung), LKC Demo Farm (Lampung), and Peusangan corridor model (Aceh) create permanent on-farm infrastructure — shade trees, riparian buffers, certified nurseries, and compost facilities — that continue without grant support. CSA practices embedded through the Change Maker network become standard farming methods, reducing long-term input costs through biological agents (Trichoderma, PGPR, Metarhizium) and on-farm compost production. The MRV system and traceability platform are transferred as operational infrastructure to consortium partners at project close, with documented open-access protocols enabling continued use. Key sustained targets: 3,000 farm plots permanently EUDR-compliant; 1,126 tCO₂e carbon sequestration by 2030 growing to 12,470 tCO₂e by 2037 as planted trees mature; nine nurseries as self-financing enterprises.
Economic - Binding green sourcing agreements with Verstegen (pepper) and Sucafina (coffee) — anchored to their B Corp certification, SBTi targets, EUDR obligations, and living income commitments — become self-executing commercial contracts creating permanent premium payment flows independent of EU funding. The LFA mechanism transitions to a local financial intermediary, sustained by buyer premiums and DFI co-investment targeting EUR 300,000 by 2030 (scalable to EUR 1,000,000 by 2032). PT CAN and PT KGF Spice/Village Hub networks are financially self-sustaining through seedling sales, compost revenue, and aggregation services. Key sustained target: 60% of trained farmers with documented income increase by 2030.
Social - Community by-laws and ILM forums create permanent governance structures under Indonesia's Village Law. 18 forum sessions build institutional memory, documented procedures, and trained facilitators. At least 20% women and 15% youth included in governance, training, and enterprise activities build a generation of community leaders in sustainable land and supply chain management. Change Makers remain as permanently trained community agronomists after project closure.
Policy and regulatory - Six landscape plans endorsed by local authorities and the policy alignment report documenting consistency with FOLU Net Sink 2030, updated NDC, and RPJMN 2025–2029 provide a replicable governance template for other Indonesian districts and commodities. Active coordination with the Ministry of Environment and Forestry, Ministry of Agriculture, and BAPPENAS increases the probability that ILM, MRV, and LFA frameworks are institutionalised within national policy instruments. EUDR-compliant traceability and MRV create permanent compliance infrastructure that becomes the de facto standard for other operators in the same landscapes.
 
2.1.4.2 Risk Analysis and Contingency Plan
 
 
2.1.4.3  Multiplier Effects, Replication, and Knowledge Sharing
aGROWforests+ is designed as a replication-ready, documented system rather than a location-specific project. At its core is a four-pillar integrated model that can be applied to any tropical commodity landscape where deforestation, weak governance, missing market incentives, and unverifiable performance claims co-exist. Pillar A (landscape governance) establishes the enabling institutional environment through ILM forums, participatory land-use plans, and community by-laws, without which on-farm change cannot be sustained at scale. Pillar B (nature-positive value chains and agroforestry) creates the on-farm and market change through CSA training, demo farms, nurseries, and EUDR-compliant traceability linked to binding green sourcing agreements with EU buyers. Pillar C (results-based finance) bridges the gap between technical adoption and economic viability by linking verified environmental performance directly to farmer payments and blended DFI investment, making nature integrated into the business case rather than a cost. Pillar D (digital MRV) provides the evidence infrastructure that makes all three other pillars credible, auditable, and investor-ready. The four pillars are mutually reinforcing: governance without market incentives reverts; market incentives without governance are captured by bad actors; finance without MRV is unverifiable; MRV without governance and market linkages generates data nobody acts on. It is the combination — and the sequencing — that makes the model transformative rather than incremental, and it is precisely this integrated architecture that makes it replicable across commodities, landscapes, and countries with minimal adaptation.

Geographic replication within and outside Indonesia: The four-pillar ILM framework is applicable to any Indonesian tropical commodity landscape with a governance gap. Models documented for Bangka/Belitung pepper, Lampung pepper and cocoa, and Aceh coffee provide commodity-specific templates directly applicable to South Sumatra, Bengkulu, and Kalimantan districts, particularly for palm oil and rubber facing EUDR exposure. The ILM framework can also be applicable outside Indonesia throughout Asia. 
Commodity extension: The EUDR-compliant traceability architecture, deforestation analysis protocol, and ecosystem services scoring framework are commodity-agnostic and directly adoptable for palm oil, rubber, and cocoa supply chains entering EU markets.
Regional cross-fertilisation: Vietnam (coffee), Malaysia (palm oil), and the Philippines (cocoa) face analogous governance, traceability, and investor-readiness challenges. The LFA mechanism design — TNFD/SBTN aligned — provides a transferable template. Dissemination through Fairfood's ISEAL membership, Sucafina's global coffee network, and WWF's Southeast Asia regional programme, plus at least two SWITCH-Asia knowledge events and one EU-Indonesia stakeholder event in Jakarta and/or Brussels.
Private sector leverage: Verstegen and Sucafina's public sourcing commitments create demonstration effects for other EU importers of Indonesian commodities. Each EUR 1 of EU grant is designed to catalyse EUR 0.10+ in private sector co-financing, with the LFA mechanism targeting EUR 300,000–1,000,000 in DFI and impact investor co-investment.
Open-access knowledge products: policy alignment report (Output 1.2), LFA mechanism design document (Output 3.1), MRV system documentation (Output 4.1), annual impact report, and programme video — all published on agrowforests.id and through SWITCH-Asia policy platforms.
2.1.4.4.  Sustainability After Completion
Financial Sustainability No outcome is structurally dependent on continued EU grant funding. Four independent post-grant revenue streams sustain operations: (1) Binding green sourcing agreements with Verstegen and Sucafina create permanent premium payment flows from EU buyers to Indonesian farmers, anchored to EUDR, SBTi, and living income obligations. (2) PT CAN and PT KGF Hub networks, nurseries (3,000–5,000 seedlings per village across eight villages), and composting facilities (20 tonnes per six-month cycle) are profitable at current market prices. (3) The LFA mechanism transitions to a local financial intermediary with DFI co-funding providing multi-year payment capacity. (4) Fairfood/Solidaridad maintains the traceability and MRV platform as a commercial EUDR compliance service under a cost-sharing agreement with supply chain partners.
Institutional Sustainability Each structural element has an explicit transfer point and named responsible party: ILM forums transferred to local government co-chairmanship by Month 30, with WWF Indonesia providing ongoing backstop through its Sumatra programmes; traceability and MRV systems transferred to PT CAN, PT KGF, and Sucafina as operational users with Fairfood/Solidaridad as platform maintainer under a service agreement before project closure; green sourcing agreements become self-executing commercial contracts; the finance mechanism is managed by a designated local financial intermediary identified during Act. 3.1.2; and Spice/Village Hubs, nurseries, and compost units are owned and operated by community organisations and partner companies as core business.
Policy-Level Sustainability All landscape plans, MRV protocols, and governance instruments are designed for compatibility with Indonesia's FOLU Net Sink 2030, updated NDC, RPJMN 2025–2029, EUDR, and TNFD/SBTN frameworks. The policy alignment report formally documents this compatibility, providing a model for national adoption. Ministry coordination during implementation builds institutional familiarity with the programme's models, increasing the probability of incorporation into national planning instruments. Green sourcing agreements establish documented sustainability standards (deforestation-free, living income, agroforestry adoption) for pepper, coffee, and cocoa supply chains that other buyers sourcing from the same landscapes are likely to reference, contributing to de facto sector norms.
Environmental Sustainability The programme generates substantial, compounding positive environmental impacts. Agroforestry sequestration: 1,126 tCO₂e by 2030, growing to 12,470 tCO₂e by 2037 as shade trees mature. ILM governance and landscape plans establish enforceable zoning protecting conservation areas, water catchments, and ecological corridors — including WWF's PECI Sumatran elephant corridor in Aceh. Annual EUDR-compliant deforestation monitoring through the MRV system provides permanent landscape surveillance. Riparian buffers, water retention infrastructure, and on-farm biological agent programmes deliver cumulative soil and water health benefits that are self-reinforcing. No material negative environmental effects are anticipated: the CRA and participatory design process screen all species and practices against 20-year climate projections; restoration targets genuinely degraded land; and landscape plans include explicit conservation zone protections that strengthen, rather than displace, existing social forestry and community conservation arrangements

Sucafina Indonesia | Co-applicant | Indonesia | Sucafina is a leading international farm-to-roaster coffee company headquartered in Switzerland, with a strong focus on sustainability. In Central Aceh and Medan, Sucafina focuses on Arabica coffee sourcing and processing operations. Direct supply chain with 1,000+ farmers in Central Aceh, implementing geolocation traceability, EUDR deforestation analysis, GAP training, seedling distribution, and IMS capacity building. Field agronomist team based in the region. Sustainability framework aligned with SBTi, living income, and forest conservation goals. In 2025, Sucafina coordinated emergency relief to flood-affected farmers and mill workers, demonstrating strong community relationships and adaptive capacity.
WWF Indonesia | Co-applicant | Indonesia | Conservation NGO with deep expertise in Integrated Landscape Management (ILM), climate safeguards, and community-based natural resource management. Leads landscape governance, multi-actor ILM forums, land-use planning, Climate Risk Assessment, and tenure mapping. Brings the Landscape Finance Approach (LFA) aligned with TNFD and SBTN frameworks. WWF's work in the Peusangan corridor (Aceh) — integrating elephant habitat conservation with coffee agroforestry — demonstrates the ILM approach's capacity to reconcile conservation and livelihoods in exactly the programme area. WWF also works in Lampung on implementation of the Hamurni traceability system to accommodate the NTFP supply chain and increasing its supply chain visibility.
Verstegen Spices & Sauces Asia | Co-applicant | Indonesia | Dutch family business established in 1886, achieving B Corp certification in early 2025 and SBTi-validated climate targets. Deploys blockchain transparency for fully traceable supply chains.Secures EU market demand, supports consumer-facing impact claims, and anchors EU buyer alignment with green sourcing requirements and EUDR compliance.
WWF Netherlands | Affiliate Entity | Netherlands | WWF Netherlands serves as the European institutional anchor for WWF Indonesia's landscape governance work within the consortium. WWF Netherlands draws on its established relationships with Dutch financial institutions and European impact investors to support the mobilisation of (blended) finance.

IF all four outcomes are achieved — (1) Participatory landscape governance established in six communities, aligning local land-use decisions with national climate/forest policy directions and enabling long-term SCP investment connected to spices, coffee and cocoa supply chains to the EU; (2) Value-chain actors (5,000 pepper, cocoa and coffee farmers, MSMEs, processors/exporters, EU buyers) adopt nature-positive sourcing models, supported by practical traceability and performance requirements; (3) (Blended) finance mechanisms link verified environmental performance to payments and financial access that strengthens household income resilience and support community level landscape implementation; and (4) A practical digital MRV system generates reliable climate, biodiversity, and land-use data to support implementation, enable transparent results-based payments, and inform supply chain decision-making — AND the assumptions at outcome level hold (authorities sustain policy direction; buyers sustain green sourcing demand; communities actively participate; minimum MRV operational conditions maintained) THEN the action will contribute to the overall impact: to support a transition to nature-positive, low-carbon agroforestry landscapes in Sumatra supplying deforestation-free, resource-efficient pepper, coffee and cocoa to EU markets, while increasing smallholder incomes and resilience BECAUSE The combination of ILM governance, EUDR-compliant traceable supply chains, financial reward systems for verified performance, and credible MRV creates the conditions for self-sustaining, market-driven landscape stewardship. Evidence from both predecessor programmes shows that when any two of these conditions are missing, adoption reverts once project support ends. The presence of all four is the mechanism that makes the transition durable.

Gap identified in existing pilots | How aGROWforests+ bridges it | Primary pillar
Farm-level agroforestry without landscape governance: surrounding monocultures maintain flood/landslide risk even where individual farms improve | ILM forums, Climate Risk Assessments, participatory land-use plans, and community by-laws align the whole landscape — not just individual farms | A: Landscape Governance
No durable market incentive: farmers adopt practices during project but revert without credible, long-term buyer demand | Green sourcing agreements with Verstegen (pepper) and Sucafina (coffee) anchored by EUDR obligations, B Corp/SBTi commitments, and farm-to-fork traceability create permanent commercial pull | B: Nature-Positive Value Chains
No finance mechanism: public grant covers adoption costs but no structure to sustain investment, reward performance or attract private capital | Results-based finance mechanism (ecosystem services scoring, buyer premiums, blended DFI finance, voluntary carbon/biodiversity credits) ensures nature is integrated into the business case | C: Results-Based Finance
No verified evidence: impact claims unverifiable, EUDR compliance undemonstrable, results-based payments impossible without credible data | Digital MRV system (GHG, biodiversity, land use, income) with low-tech tools for low-connectivity contexts provides the evidence layer for all other pillars | D: Digital MRV

Role | Exporter and implementation organisation in Bangka Belitung
Donor | GIZ (Initiative for Climate Smart Supply Chains – I4C)
Amount contributed by donor | EUR 2,000,000
Dates | December 2022 – May 2027
Objectives & Results | Objective: Contribute to a sustainable and climate-resilient spice sector in Bangka Belitung by increasing pepper farmers' productivity and income through climate smart agriculture (CSA) and agroforestry. Results: 1,344 farmers trained in CSA and agroforestry; 10 Spice Hubs established across Bangka and Belitung as community-based centers for training, trading, and agroforestry transition; 658 farmers enabled to trade pepper with PT CAN through Spice Hub networks; 250.65 hectares farmed using nature-positive practices; approximately 46,951 seedlings distributed (including 24,501 pepper seedlings) to 223 farmers; 132.4 tonnes of compost distributed to 308 farmers; Koperasi Aik Ladan Indonesia legally established in Belitung; 946 TRACE cards printed and distributed for farmers traceability; and a project dashboard and website developed for monitoring and knowledge management.

Project Title | aGROWforests for sustainable spices in Indonesia
Sector | Sustainable Agriculture / Value Chains / SCP / Agroforestry
Location | Lampung and Bangka/Belitung, Sumatra, Indonesia
Amount (EUR) | EUR 4,000,000
Role | EU market anchor, co-initiator, main co-financer
Donor | GIZ / I4C
Amount contributed by donor | EUR 2,000,000
Dates | December 2022 – May 2027
Objectives & Results | Objectives: Source 100% deforestation-free Indonesian pepper by December 2025 and ensure 50% sustainably grown agricultural raw materials by 2030 (Net Zero by 2050). B Corp certified (early 2025); SBTi-validated climate targets; 90% Scope 1+2 reduction by 2035.   Results: Blockchain transparency for traceable supply chains. Spice Hub co-establishment with Fairfood. Farmer-centric sourcing approach moving away from bulk buying toward deep ecological and technological integration. Safeguard farming communities from price fluctuations by introducing layered cash crops to diversify farmer’s income. Financial fairness by paying premium prices directly to smallholder farmers for sustainably and traceably produced white and black pepper. This framework moves away from volatile commodity pricing to provide a predictable financial incentive for regenerative practices.
Co-applicant: WWF Indonesia

Project Title | Reinventing The Land Use Governance In Development Across Sumatera
Sector | Landscape Governance / Sustainable Land Use
Location | Sumatra, Indonesia
Amount (EUR) | GBP 250,000
Role | Lead implementer for landscape governance component
Donor | FCDO-Multistakeholder Forestry Programme (MFP5)
Amount contributed by donor | GBP 350,000
Dates | 2026 – 2027
Objectives & Results | Objective: The objective of the Reinventing the Land Use Governance in Development Across Sumatera project is to redesign Sumatra’s land-use system to support healthy and productive ecosystems, safeguard globally and locally important biodiversity, and deliver sustainable benefits for communities through coordinated action among government, the private sector, and local stakeholders. To bridge this governance framework with market-driven incentives, the project will specifically design and pilot a transparent traceability model for NTFP coffee supply chains, supporting sustainable and deforestation-free production and explore scalable opportunities for integration with national sustainability initiatives.
