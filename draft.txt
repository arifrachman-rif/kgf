S E C T I O N 0 3
Background and Problem
The three target landscapes share one structural condition: forest, smallholder farmland, and mining concessions overlap on the same ground. In Bangka Belitung, extensive kolong (former tin-mining pits) and continuing mining pressure draw young labour away from farms. In Lampung, robusta-coffee households repeatedly fall back on artisanal gold mining as a survival strategy, leaving chemical residues in the soil. In southern West Java, illegal gold mining and highland vegetable monoculture erode strategic upper watersheds.
The cost registers on two ledgers at once. One is the erosion of the FOLU carbon sink, which runs counter to Indonesia’s NDC and its FOLU Net Sink 2030 commitment. The other is a livelihoods gap: land that remains productive is not productive enough, or not bankable enough, to compete with the extractive economy next to it. Conventional after-the-fact reclamation of degraded land is slow and expensive, and often fails economically because no market awaits its output.
The programme intervenes at the more effective point, which lies with the land that is still productive rather than with land already lost. Where standing forest, smallholder gardens, and coastal mangrove are managed as one productive mosaic and connected to real markets, the same hectares can carry both a higher household income and a measurable carbon contribution. Mining is treated here as context that motivates the problem, not as the organising axis of the design.
S E C T I O N 0 4
Landscape-Based Approach
This is the organising idea of the programme. The programme manages a multi-functional landscape, from standing forest to smallholder gardens and coastal mangrove — and rather than treating a farm plot, a forest block, and a mangrove belt as separate projects, it manages them as one land–coast mosaic, extending to the shoreline and stopping before open water. Agroforestry is the livelihood-based model through which this landscape is managed regeneratively. Carbon and value are accounted at the level of that whole mosaic, not plot by plot.
Why the landscape unit matters.
Boundary definition. The landscape boundary is not asserted at concept stage. It is defined during the Year-1 baseline, combining forest-management-unit (KPH) areas, district (kabupaten) administration, watershed (DAS) hydrology, and the cluster of Social Forestry villages. All scale ranges in this note flow from that delineation.
Governance. Multi-party landscape governance (farmers, KPH, and local government) is facilitated by the consortium, not imposed. A landscape forum convenes on a boundary-aligned membership and documents its decisions.
Ownership of the landscape layer.
Because FOLU accounting is done at the landscape level across the mosaic, the additionality and permanence arguments that third-party verification and national FOLU Net Sink accounting require rest on a stronger and more auditable footing than any single-parcel claim could provide.
S E C T I O N 0 5
Theory of Change
Framing. The programme manages one land–coast landscape as a whole mosaic. Inside that frame, three value- chain functions operate (Produce, Connect and Enable, Transform) over a binding layer of landscape governance and MRV. The theory of change is that moving farmers' point of value capture up the value ladder (raw material, to intermediate, to high-value compound) while keeping the landscape mosaic standing produces two interlocking impacts: (A) farmer welfare rises, and (B) a verified FOLU carbon contribution is generated. These two impacts, A and B, are referenced throughout this note, including the "Serves impact" column below.
Causal chain. 
"Serves impact" key: A = farmer welfare; B = verified landscape-level FOLU carbon; A + B = the governance lock that secures both.
Key mechanism. Produce raises the volume and quality of raw material; Connect links it to institutions, traceability, financing, and contracted off-take; Transform raises the value level (raw to compound). Each rung of the ladder that is climbed is a larger share of value captured by farmers, which is Impact A. In parallel, agroforestry on degraded land plus protected standing forest plus restored mangrove constitutes a net sink, measured by landscape-level MRV and taken to third-party verification, which is Impact B.
The cross-lock (why the two impacts are one system). Prosperous farmers have an economic reason to keep the mosaic standing, which secures the permanence that Impact B requires. In the other direction, the connecting (midstream) system that sustains farmer welfare is financed within the grant period by programmatic NC funding and sustained after it by commercial value-chain revenue, with potential NEK/SPE-GRK incentives as a conditional supplement under a single-claim discipline; the programme’s verified contribution, in turn, strengthens the case for continued NC allocations to the landscape. Landscape governance (OC5) is the gate that locks the two together.
Funding and contribution logic. Impact A and Impact B are framed as equal and interlocking. The programme is financed programmatically under the NC Investment Plan: grant funding is disbursed up front against an approved proposal and Annual Work Plan, not paid per tonne of carbon. The verified carbon result (Impact B) is therefore not a unit the programme sells; it is the programme’s measurable, auditable contribution to the national FOLU Net Sink result — the result that underpins Norway’s contribution to Indonesia (see §10). Farmer welfare (Impact A) functions as the pathway of change and as the co-benefit that secures permanence, which is what makes the carbon contribution durable and credible. Equal in the narrative; the verified carbon is the programme’s measurable contribution to the national result.
S E C T I O N 0 6
Programme Components (by the three functions)
The value chain is named by function (Produce, Connect and Enable, Transform). Product value level (raw, intermediate, pure compound) is a value ladder within Transform. Off-take is always a Connect function, whatever rung of product is being sold.
Produce (upstream) OC1
Multi-strata agroforestry built on a multi-commodity portfolio rather than a single crop, beginning from what already grows and is familiar to farmers, then raising its class agronomically. The portfolio spans pepper, coffee (robusta and Priangan arabica), patchouli, cocoa, cinnamon, nutmeg, cloves, bamboo (West Java), and ginger and other rhizomes, spices and herbs. It is a model adaptive to each landscape, not a fixed planting recipe, and pepper is one commodity among many.

The multi-commodity, multi-strata design gives income diversification (Impact A) and a net sink (contribution to B) on the same land. Climbing crops such as the pepper liana require a vertical living support, so across the portfolio the agroforestry logic and the carbon logic reinforce each other by construction rather than by rhetoric.
Connect and Enable (midstream) OC2
This function is the heart of value flow. It turns thousands of dispersed farmers into one bankable, off-takeable counterparty with verified origin, then locks demand through contracts and channels working capital.
The institutional pathway is KUPS to UMKM, not cooperative. KUPS rise into formal UMKM units under the Cluster- based MSME Holding sector #4; a UMKM shell without institutional readiness is a risk, not an output.
Transform (downstream) OC3
Simple extraction rising toward high-value compounds moves the product from a commodity market (price-taker) to a specialty-ingredient market (specification-driven). The value ladder lives here.

6.4 Financing
Pepper agroforestry farmer groups today are trapped in high-cost, unsustainable informal financing (debt bondage/middlemen lending). The goal of this component is to shift the financing source from informal schemes toward a structured local mechanism linked to national instruments.
6.5 Strengthening Stakeholders’ Capacity 
Pepper agroforestry stakeholders today rely on fragmented, informally transmitted knowledge with no standardized quality control across sites. The goal of this component is to shift capacity from individual, ad-hoc know-how toward institutionalized, replicable skill systems that outlast the project.

S E C T I O N 0 9
MRV and Carbon Logic
Carbon claims are made at the landscape level, across the whole mosaic (standing forest, agroforestry, and mangrove) rather than parcel by parcel. This is what gives the additionality and permanence arguments an auditable footing.
Methodology by carbon pool. The MRV is built from complementary methods matched to each carbon pool. Agrivision supplies the soil-carbon (SOC) and agroforestry MRV layer — compliance-grade SOC and biomass measurement (satellite/UAV plus AI/ML modelling) with farm-level traceability through a Farm Identification Code (FID) plot registry, taken to standard-aligned reporting that is ready for independent third-party validation. The standing-forest and mangrove (blue-carbon) components are measured with complementary forest- and coastal-carbon methods appropriate to those pools. The layers are consolidated into one landscape-level account, nested into national FOLU accounting.
MRV stands for Measurement, Reporting and Verification. The programme builds it in a deliberately conservative sequence. Within 2027–2029 the target is to set the baseline, run operational MRV, and have the first third-party verification cycle begin (verifier engaged and protocol ready), rather than to complete a verification cycle inside the period; the closing of the first cycle falls beyond the period. The function of verification here is to provide auditable evidence of the programme’s contribution to national FOLU Net Sink accounting — nested into the national registry (SRN) and measured against the national reference level (FREL) — not to generate carbon units for sale.
Carbon targets are set after the Year-1 baseline; no tCO₂e figures are asserted at concept stage. Additionality rests on the landscape counterfactual and on measured net sink; permanence rests on the economic incentive for farmers to keep the mosaic standing (the cross-lock in §5).
S E C T I O N 1 0
Financing
The organising principle is that capital is disbursed against an approved plan and accounted against verified results. Each instrument is tied to a plan, a milestone, or a real result (an approved Annual Work Plan, a signed off- take contract, an institutional graduation, a verified landscape contribution), not to a nominal amount to be spent.
Funding mechanics. The programme’s primary in-period funding is a programmatic grant under the Norway’s Contribution (NC) Investment Plan. In this mechanism, an implementing partner submits a proposal and an Annual Work Plan (AWP); once the proposal passes verification, funds are disbursed up front to run the approved activities, monitored quarterly and annually against the AWP. The funds are managed by BPDLH / the Indonesian Environment Fund (IEF) as fund manager under the Ministry of Finance, with Kemenhut holding programme responsibility. For context, the Results-Based Contribution (RBC) operates one layer above this programme: it is the state-to-state mechanism under which Norway contributes to Indonesia after verified reductions in national emissions against the national reference level (FREL), under the 2022 Indonesia–Norway FOLU partnership (contributions to date of roughly USD 216 million). This programme is not paid per tonne; it is financed programmatically and delivers a measurable, auditable contribution to the national result on which that partnership rests.
Execution capacity as programme value. Disbursement of NC funds to the field has so far run well behind the funds Indonesia has received — on the order of ten per cent (~9.7%) of roughly Rp 2.91 trillion received. The binding constraint on the domestic layer is execution capacity, not capital. A consortium that arrives execution-ready — with contracted off-take, an operating traceability platform, tied working capital, and a decade of purchasing history — is built to convert an approved AWP into delivered, monitorable activity from Year 1.
The financing cross-lock, without circularity. Programmatic NC funding builds the Produce, Connect, and MRV systems within the grant period. Post-grant, the Connect (midstream) system is sustained by commercial value- chain revenue, with potential NEK/SPE-GRK incentives as a conditional supplement. Prosperous farmers keep the mosaic standing, which secures permanence; the programme’s verified contribution to the national FOLU Net Sink result, in turn, strengthens the case for continued NC allocations to the landscape. Welfare secures the carbon; the demonstrated contribution sustains the funding case.
Indicative budget. Rp 70–100 billion over three years (2027–2029), indicative and finalised after the Year-1 landscape assessment. The lower bound corresponds to a phased start (Babel and West Java first, Lampung in a later phase); the upper bound to all three landscapes at full scale from year one. Inter-layer allocation, continued off-take-advance size, impact-investor ticket, and the scale of any NEK/SPE-GRK incentive are held until the finance scheme and baseline are locked. The impact-investor layer is a candidate (Acces Capital under consideration); no Islamic-finance instruments are used in this version.
S E C T I O N 1 1
Governance and Delivery
Steering. Bappenas, through the Directorate of Forestry and Water Resources Conservation, is the policy convener. Kemenhut holds programme responsibility for FOLU Net Sink implementation; BPDLH/IEF, under the Ministry of Finance, manages the NC funds. The Steering Committee meets at least quarterly with a standing agenda: milestone review, tranche-allocation decisions, and resolution of inter-partner escalations.
Executing Consortium. The core institutions operate under a governance charter that fixes veto rights at outcome level, quarterly co-audit, a RACI matrix per outcome, and performance-based tranche disbursement to prevent stalling at the MoU stage.
Landscape forum. At the landscape level, the multi-party forum (farmers, KPH, local government) convenes on a boundary-aligned membership, facilitated by the consortium, and documents its governance decisions (OC5).
Social-Forestry roles run on two tiers to avoid overlap: YKAN holds landscape-level conservation oversight (standards, safeguards, MRV assurance) while Titian leads on-the-ground Social Forestry delivery (KUPS strengthening, FPIC facilitation, community organising) under that oversight.
Endorsement-to-execution flow. Bappenas issues a policy recommendation; Kemenhut, as programme-responsible ministry, endorses the consortium’s implementing-partner proposal under the NC Investment Plan; funds flow , disbursed against the approved Annual Work Plan in monitored tranches. The consortium reports quarterly to the Steering Committee and the PMU, and annually to the public (FOLU transparency).
Pentahelix principle. The five-actor configuration is realised through consortium composition: Kemenhut, Bappenas, Kemen UMKM, and Kemnaker (Government); CAN, KGF, and EBM (Business); EBM’s academic partners (Academia); YKAN, Titian, TLE, and KUPS/UMKM (Community); and programme communication through donor and public channels (Media).

S E C T I O N 1 3
Risks and Assumptions
Each assumption below is load-bearing: if it collapses, a link in the Theory of Change collapses. Each carries a mitigation.
External factors under monitoring, beyond the programme’s control: global commodity prices; cross-cabinet policy continuity after 2029; EUDR regulation and EU trade conditions; and fluctuations in local mining demand.
S E C T I O N 1 4
Exit and Sustainability
The programme is designed to stand on three legs, all of which must be upright by Year 3.
Leg 1 — Commercial. Verstegen and other premium off-take contracts operate at commercial volume; CAN and KGF serve as long-term commercial anchors; a floor price plus volume floor locks in a minimum household income.
Leg 2 — Institutional. KUPS graduate into formal UMKM under the Cluster-based MSME Holding sector #4, owned by their members and able to access commercial credit and the KUR programme; Kemnaker-certified agripreneurs serve as the management cadre. The pathway is KUPS to UMKM, not to a cooperative.
Leg 3 — Carbon-linked incentives and continued allocation. The programme’s verified landscape contribution supports two conditional channels: potential domestic NEK/SPE-GRK incentives, subject to SRN registration and a strict single-claim discipline with the national FOLU Net Sink result, and a strengthened case for continued programmatic allocations under the NC framework. A share of any incentive returns to villages and UMKM through a benefit-sharing mechanism agreed by the consortium; landscape MRV continues on a fee-for-service basis.
Post-grant governance. Bappenas and Kemenhut remain institutional patrons; the consortium transitions from a project consortium to a standing bioeconomy alliance under a permanent charter; the funder relationship shifts from a time-bound grant toward a standing programmatic partnership within the national FOLU Net Sink architecture.
Year-3 gate. A majority of mentored village cash flow derived from off-take, downstream processing, and carbon- linked incentives rather than grant funding; KUPS graduated into UMKM; the Holding operating with registered transaction volume; the consortium signing a permanent governance charter.
S E C T I O N 1 5
Timeline
Three years (2027–2029), sequenced per landscape and per function, with the carbon track kept deliberately conservative.
Post-grant horizon: a maturing productive climax system, a continuing verified carbon contribution to the national FOLU Net Sink result, and upper-watershed environmental services, with the closing of the first verification cycle falling beyond the grant period.
